When condemned by a colonial military tribunal on charges of rebellion, sedition, and illegal association, Dr. José Rizal did not resort to polemics or impassioned melodrama. Instead, he deployed an exacting, forensic legal mind—drafting Adiciones a Mi Defensa to dismantle the prosecution’s circumstantial allegations point by rigorous point.

1861 – 1882

Early Formative Years & UST

Calamba • Ateneo Municipal • Univ. of Santo Tomas

Born June 19, 1861 in Calamba, Laguna. Early witness to Spanish administrative injustices and the 1872 GOMBURZA executions. Completed Bachiller en Artes at Ateneo and commenced medical and surveying studies at UST before sailing for Spain.

Foundation of Intellectual Defense
1882 – 1892

European Propaganda Movement & Dual Masterpieces

Madrid • Paris • Berlin • Brussels • Ghent

Authored Noli Me Tángere (1887) and El Filibusterismo (1891). Alongside fellow propagandists Marcelo H. del Pilar (editor of La Solidaridad) and Graciano López Jaena (founding editor of La Solidaridad), Rizal advocated for Philippine representation in the Spanish Cortes, civil liberties, and secular educational reform. He also annotated Antonio de Morga's Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas (1890) to document pre-colonial Filipino civilization.

Reform Through Law & Education
July 1892 – July 1896

Founding of La Liga Filipina & 4-Year Dapitan Exile

Ilaya St., Tondo • Dapitan, Zamboanga

Founded La Liga Filipina on July 3, 1892, as a peaceful civic league dedicated to mutual aid and industrial development. Arrested three days later by Gov. Gen. Despujol and exiled to Dapitan. For four years, he practiced medicine, taught youth, engaged in agriculture, and abstained from political agitation.

Crucial Precedent for Points 1, 4, 6, 7 & 11
August – October 1896

Katipunan Uprising & The Cuba Physician Mission

Manila Bay (Cruiser Castilla) • Mediterranean Transit

Granted permission by Gov. Gen. Ramón Blanco to serve as a volunteer military physician in yellow-fever stricken Cuba. While awaiting passage aboard the Castilla, the Katipunan rebellion erupted in August 1896. Despite pleas from friends to flee during his layover in Singapore (where British law protected political refugees), Rizal refused and continued his voyage. Upon docking at Barcelona on October 3, 1896 aboard the Isla de Panay, he was immediately arrested and confined at Montjuïc Castle, then transferred to the transport Colón and returned to Manila as a state prisoner.

Basis for Points 3 & 4 (Clear Conscience)
November 3 – December 12, 1896

Imprisonment, Choosing Counsel & Writing Data for My Defense

Fort Santiago Prison Cell, Manila

Returned to Manila aboard the Colón on November 3, 1896, Rizal was imprisoned at Fort Santiago and subjected to a preliminary investigation. Allowed to choose his defender only from a list of Spanish army officers, he selected Lt. Luis Taviel de Andrade—brother of José Taviel de Andrade, his bodyguard in 1887. On December 12, 1896, he wrote Data for My Defense: a private memorandum giving his lawyer the facts on the rebellion, La Liga Filipina, and Masonry.

December 26, 1896

Court-Martial & Writing the 12-Point Defense

Cuartel de España, Intramuros (Trial) • Fort Santiago (Prison)

Tried before a Spanish military court presided by Lt. Col. José Togores Polo at the Cuartel de España (Intramuros). Rizal was held at Fort Santiago and denied the right to confront or cross-examine witnesses. With only an assigned military defender (Lt. Luis Taviel de Andrade, 1st Lt. of Artillery), he submitted Adiciones a Mi Defensa—a 12-point supplement refuting each charge, establishing that he was named an involuntary figurehead without his knowledge or consent. The verdict was effectively predetermined: Governor General Polavieja had already decided the sentence before the proceedings concluded.

The Subject of this Micro-Site
December 29, 1896

Final Hours: The Retraction Controversy & Josephine Bracken

Fort Santiago Prison Cell, Manila

The eve of execution is one of the most contested periods in Philippine historiography. Two events remain actively debated by scholars:

  • The Retraction (Historically Disputed): A document purportedly signed by Rizal renouncing Masonry and returning to the Catholic faith was discovered in archdiocesan archives in 1935—39 years after his death. While Jesuit witnesses (Fr. Vicente Balaguer) affirm its authenticity, many historians cite contradictions in the document's versions and its 39-year disappearance as evidence of fabrication. The matter remains unresolved.
  • Marriage to Josephine Bracken: Rizal's Irish-Filipino companion, who had lived with him during his Dapitan exile, is said to have been married to him in a Catholic ceremony on the morning of December 30, 1896 at Fort Santiago. Because of the retraction controversy, the authenticity of this marriage is also debated—Catholic canon law would have required the retraction first. Josephine herself gave conflicting testimony over the years.
⚖ Historiographical Note: Neither event was mentioned in Adiciones a Mi Defensa (Dec 26), which was focused solely on his legal defense arguments.

December 30, 1896

Execution at Bagumbayan (Luneta)

Bagumbayan Field, Manila (7:03 AM)

Gov. Gen. Camilo de Polavieja signed the death warrant. Rizal requested to face the firing squad—denied, as traitors were shot in the back. He fell forward and managed to turn his body to face the sky at the moment of death. He was 35 years old. The day before (December 29), he had secretly passed Mi Último Adiós—written on a small scrap of paper folded into quarters—to his sister Trinidad, hidden inside a small alcohol cooking stove (cocinilla), whispering in English: "There is something inside." The poem was retrieved with a hairpin after his execution.

The End of the Thinker, The Dawn of Nationhood
PRIVATE MEMORANDUM TO COUNSEL • 12 DICIEMBRE 1896

Data for My Defense: Rizal’s Notes to His Defender

Two weeks before the Adiciones, Rizal wrote this memorandum in his cell at Fort Santiago for the use of his defense attorney. It is the raw material of his defense — candid, personal, and argued in his own words.

Date Written December 12, 1896 14 days before the Adiciones
Written In His Prison Cell Fort Santiago, Manila
Intended Reader His Defense Attorney Lt. Luis Taviel de Andrade
Form of the Document Private Memorandum Rebellion • Liga • Masonry • Closing

A § I
With Regard to the Rebellion

Part A: Valenzuela’s Visit — “Don’t You Think of Me, but of the Country”

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

I had no knowledge that a rebellion was being planned until the 1st or 2nd July 1896 when Pío Valenzuela came to see me telling me about an uprising. I told him that it was absurd, etc. etc., and he replied that they could no longer endure the situation. I advised him to have patience, etc. etc. He added then that they had sent him to me because they pity my life and that probably they will impute it1 to me. I replied that I would be patient and that if they did something to me, I would then prove my innocence. As to the rest, I added, “Don’t you think of me but of the country which is going to suffer.” And I again explained to him how absurd the movement was. This was admitted by Pío Valenzuela afterwards. He did not tell me that they would use my name or mention to me that I be leader or something like that.

Defense Significance

Rizal fixes the exact moment of his first knowledge of the uprising and rests it on the prosecution’s own witness: Valenzuela himself “admitted” the conversation. The emissary was not asking for orders; he came to warn Rizal that the blame would fall on him. Rizal’s answer — be patient, think of the country — is the factual seed of Point 1 of the Adiciones.

Cross-Reference to the Adiciones: Developed further in Adiciones Point 1
B § II
With Regard to the Rebellion • Burden of Proof

Part B: “Where Is the Leader Who Does Not Issue Any Order?”

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

Those who declared that I am the leader (whom I don’t know nor do I believe I have met them) should present some proof that I have accepted that leadership, or that I had relations with them or with their association! Either they have made use of my name for their own ends, or they have been deceived by those men. Where is the leader who does not issue any order or any rule, who is not consulted at all about so transcendental a movement, except at the eleventh hour, and when he gives his opinion against it is not obeyed? Since 1st July 1892, I have absolutely given up politics. It is obvious that they have wanted to use my name in order to do what they please.

Defense Significance

Here Rizal shifts the burden of proof back to his accusers: whoever calls him “leader” must prove he accepted that role. His rhetorical question is the first draft of the most famous line of the Adiciones — “What kind of a chief is that who, when he says ‘No’, they say ‘Yes’?” A leader who gives no orders, is not consulted, and is disobeyed is no leader at all.

Cross-Reference to the Adiciones: Sharpened into Adiciones Point 5 — “What Kind of a Chief is That?”
C § III
With Regard to the Rebellion • The Castilla

Part C: Aboard the Castilla — Offering Himself to Suppress the Rising

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

Nay, when the uprising broke out, I was on board the Castilla, incommunicado, and I offered myself unconditionally to His Excellency (a thing I had never done before) to suppress the rebellion. But this was a personal letter and it was witnessed by Colonel Santaló. This cannot be used without the permission of His Excellency.

I have always been opposed to rebellion because I was hoping Spain would give us soon liberties, as I told Pío Valenzuela, because I could see that in order to forestall future events, a very close union between Spain and the Filipino people was necessary.

Defense Significance

When the revolution broke out in August 1896, Rizal was being held incommunicado aboard the cruiser Castilla in Manila Bay — physically unable to lead anything. Note his legal care: he tells his lawyer that the letter to the Governor-General is privileged and may not be used without His Excellency’s permission. Even facing death, he instructs counsel on the proper handling of evidence.

Cross-Reference to the Adiciones: Compare Adiciones Point 3 — Cuba service & surrender
D § IV
With Regard to the Rebellion • Documentary Evidence

Part D: “Where Is One Single Letter of Mine?” — The Biscuit Jars

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

It is not true that I carried on correspondence with them; where is one single letter of mine? My correspondence all went through the provincial military commander; and if they have written me, it is easy for the letter to fall into the hands of the government, as it happened to what appears to have been written by Deodato Arellano and found in the judicial records. Deodato Arellano says that he cannot find means to send me letters. Sending me letters inside biscuit jars is curious; in Dapitan I had given away those biscuits to the people. At times the jars arrived already open; at times they were broken on the boat in loading and unloading. It is true that a man came to me but I placed him under the order of the governor who sent him to Manila.

Defense Significance

Rizal attacks the documentary case: not one letter in his hand was produced. As an exile, all his mail passed through the politico-military commander of Dapitan, so a secret channel was implausible. He even turns the prosecution’s exhibit against it — the letter attributed to Deodato Arellano (first president of the Katipunan) complains that they could not reach him. The “biscuit-jar” theory collapses on practical facts: he gave the biscuits away, and the jars often arrived open or broken.

Cross-Reference to the Adiciones: Compare Adiciones Point 2 — absence of secret correspondence
E § V
With Regard to the Liga • Liberty vs. Independence

Part E: La Liga Filipina — “To Have Liberties” Is Not “To Have Independence”

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

It is true that I wrote the statutes at the request of Mr. Basa2 and they were sent to Manila. The purposes of the Liga were union, encouragement of commerce, industry, etc., because I understood and I understand that a people cannot have liberties without having first material prosperity; that to have liberties without having food to eat is to listen to speeches and to fast. I also believed that if Spain systematically denied liberties to the Philippines, there would be insurrections and I have so written it down, deploring that such a thing might occur but not hoping for it. Well now, many have interpreted my phrase to have liberties as to have independence, which are two different things. A people can be free without being independent, and a people can be independent without being free. I have always desired liberties for the Philippines and I have said so. Others who testify that I said independence either have put the cart before the horse or they lie. Now, that I have also believed that little by little autonomy would come and finally independence is true. Spain would abandon this country when she is convinced that her future is in Morocco and that this costs her more sacrifices than anything else, and she will abandon it even when the Filipinos would like to stop her, as she had tried to do several times in past centuries. This was the meaning of what I said that it was necessary to be worthy, to be united, so that when the time came, we might not fall into the hands of Japan, or of England, or of Germany.

The Liga, for whose organization Mr. Ambrosio Salvador was elected president, did not live, dying shortly after I was deported to Dapitan. They have testified thus; I learned about it afterwards, but I already surmised it.

Defense Significance

The heart of Rizal’s political defense is a definition: liberty (rights within the Spanish state) is not independence (separation from it). Witnesses who reported “independence” either confused the two or lied. He admits candidly that he expected autonomy and eventual independence to come gradually — but by Spain’s own choice, not by force — and insists the Liga’s aim was material prosperity first: “to have liberties without having food to eat is to listen to speeches and to fast.”

Cross-Reference to the Adiciones: Compare Adiciones Points 6 & 7 — Liga’s statutes and early death
F § VI
With Regard to the Liga • The Katipunan & Ongjunco

Part F: A Revived Liga, the Katipunan, and Meetings with Strangers

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

Whether others have revived it or reorganized it, I don’t know nor do I have to be concerned about it. Neither do I know who reorganized it nor do I know what changes they had made. From the abstract I have learned that they reorganized it nine or ten months after it had died.

Neither do I know the Katipunan nor have I maintained relations or correspondence with it. Let them show two characters of mine in proof of what they claim.

I cannot understand how I could have convoked the members who met at the house of Ongjunco when I don’t know many of them nor do I know Mr. Ongjunco. To convoke persons whom I don’t know in the house of a person whom I don’t know either? It’s curious.

Pedro Serrano and Timoteo Páez, without having been invited by me, went to Malolos, Bulacan, to look for me, when I went there by train.

Defense Significance

Rizal separates himself from anything done after his deportation: the Liga was reorganized by others nine or ten months after it died, and he neither knew nor guided the changes. His challenge — “Let them show two characters of mine” — demands handwritten proof of any tie to the Katipunan. On the gathering at Doroteo Ongjunco’s house in Tondo, his point is narrow and precise: he did not convoke it and did not know its host or many of those present, so it cannot be read as his own conspiratorial network.

Cross-Reference to the Adiciones: Compare Adiciones Points 8, 9 & 12
G § VII
Masonry • La Solidaridad

Part G: Masonry, Pedro Serrano, and La Solidaridad

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

It is not true that I had given orders to Pedro Serrano to introduce Masonry in the Philippines. Serrano had a higher degree than I had in Masonry; I did not reach above the third degree, while Serrano had 30 or 33 degrees, and this is proven by the letter that afterwards he sent me when I was in Hong Kong, a letter attached to the records, in which he appointed me Venerable, like a great thing. If I were the chief, when does an officer permit himself to raise the rank of a captain general? That letter proves the falsity of the assertion.

Moreover, Serrano and I separated in Europe quite unfriendly. I left Madrid in January or February 1891 and since then I have given up writing and taking part in the management of La Solidaridad and I dropped Masonry. It is also untrue that I founded the Hispano-Philippine Association; it was already in existence long before I went to Madrid. The same is true with La Solidaridad; this was founded by Marcelo H. del Pilar3 and was always headed by him. What I founded in Madrid had no other object but to moralize the Filipinos, compel them to attend their classes, not to get into debts, etc. And when I wished to fiscalize the acts of La Solidaridad, Marcelo H. del Pilar opposed me, as confirmed by one of the letters attached to the records. This proves that the political side was never under my direction. Now, if they use my name for their purposes and in order to deceive the unwary, I could not prevent it.

Among the declarations there are some that are infamously false, like the one that mentions the names of all those who attended the meeting at the house of Ongjunco. They mentioned names of persons who could not possibly have attended it. The presence of Pedro Serrano is untrue also, as well as that of Moisés Salvador, of Timoteo Páez partly, and of others. It would be very easy to prove their falsity.

Defense Significance

A lesson in reasoning from the evidence on file. Serrano’s own letter, already in the records, conferred an honorary title on Rizal — and a subordinate does not promote his commander. Rizal then dismantles the claim that he directed the reform movement in Spain: he left Madrid in early 1891 after his rift with del Pilar, and del Pilar blocked him from overseeing La Solidaridad. Editorial note: as footnote 3 records, the paper was actually founded by Graciano López Jaena in Barcelona (1889) and later directed by del Pilar.

H § VIII
Dapitan • Himno a Talisay

Part H: Himno a Talisay — Teaching Boys to Protect Their Families

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

The poem Himno a Talisay is mine. I had boys of 12 to 14 years whom I taught Spanish, arithmetic, English, horticulture, carpentry, masonry, etc. During playtime I taught them gymnastics, fencing, and hunting with rifle the older ones. The verse which says That they will know how to protect their families alludes to the numerous incursions of the Moros who went to Dapitan and made prisoners. Many relatives of these boys had been killed or captured by the Moros. Talisay is the place where I lived with these boys. I had to support for some time the widow and children of an unfortunate man who was killed by the Moros for not being able to use the rifle he had. This is officially known.

What fault have I that they make use of my name when I could not know it or prevent it? Many also use the name of God for their ends or their passions.

Defense Significance

The prosecution read a line of Rizal’s school hymn as military training for rebels. Rizal supplies the context: these were his pupils at Talisay, Dapitan, in a frontier town exposed to Moro raids where many of their relatives had been killed or captured — a fact he says is “officially known.” His closing comparison is pointed: people also misuse the name of God for their own ends; that does not make God their accomplice.

I § IX
Union & Prosperity • The Safety Valve

Part I: Separatism Predates Me — Free Press as a “Safety Valve”

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

Let them show the statutes of the Liga and it will be seen that what I was pursuing were union, commercial and industrial development, and the like. That these things—union and money—after years could prepare for a revolution, I don’t have to deny; but they could also prevent all revolutions, because people who live comfortably and have money do not go for adventures. Moreover, I was sure that Spain would then make concessions compatible with the state of the country. I have said this in my writings.

Separatist ideas have existed in the Philippines for many many years. In this century alone there occurred many uprisings: Those of Novales, Cuesta, Apolinario,4 in the Ilocos and Pangasinan, of the regiment of the Pampangos, of Cavite and again that of Pangasinan in 1884. I described this revolutionary sentiment in a Madrid newspaper, El Progreso, in July 1884, calling the attention of the government to its causes and its future. I have asked insistently for the freedom of the press and representatives as the means to forestall uprisings, as safety or escape valves. I was greatly marked out among others because during the time that I was engaged in politics I played the part of free press and Philippine representative, and I can say that from 1884 to 1891, the year I gave up writing, there occurred neither a mutiny or attempts at it nor political deportations.

In the Philippines there is nothing that happens that is not imputed to me. While I was on board the Castilla, a rumor spread that I had been seen in Cavite with a dynamite bomb in the hand and many believed it. The commander of the Castilla told me about it, but this cannot be used without first consulting Mr. Santaló for he told me about it privately.

Defense Significance

Two arguments work together. First, causation: separatist uprisings — from Novales and Apolinario de la Cruz to Cavite — long predate Rizal, so he cannot be their origin. Second, a counter-factual record: while he wrote openly for reforms (1884–1891), there were no mutinies and no political deportations. Prosperity, he argues, prevents revolutions, “because people who live comfortably and have money do not go for adventures.” The dynamite rumor shows how readily anything was pinned on him.

J § X
★ Key Metaphor • The Pustule & the Disease

Part J: “I Am the Pustule, Not the Smallpox”

"I’m like some diseases, like smallpox. I’m the pustule through which the disease of the blood is manifested; remove the pustule and… the smallpox goes inside and kills the patient."
— Dr. José Rizal, Data for My Defense, December 12, 1896

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

Everything is ascribed to me for I have meant more than anybody else, I have been more frank in saying what I thought and never have I been a hypocrite or traitor. When I attacked the Government for deporting my brother and brothers-in-law, I did it openly. Later when I promised not to engage again in politics, I fulfilled my word, and I cut off forever my political relations. Everything is imputed to me because I have meant more, but separatist ideas are not mine; rather I am their effect. Since 1884 I have been informing the Government of the march of opinion; I asked urgently for some concessions, like representation; I revealed the abuses that were being committed; I was a safety valve. They suppressed me in 1892 through deportation, and the valve was closed. I’m like some diseases, like smallpox. I’m the pustule through which the disease of the blood is manifested; remove the pustule and, as the man in the street says, the smallpox goes inside and kills the patient. Now the ignorant and stupid physicians believe that the pustule is the smallpox or is the disease. This is what has happened. Just as one swallow does not make summer, neither can one man make a revolution. Today the predictions of Pi y Margall, Zorrilla, and Salmerón do not make a revolution in spite of the fact that the Spanish people are more bellicose, more indomitable, more nervous. I’m not more than any of these great republicans, nor do I reach their ankles. Jesus Christ, despite the fact that he had been preaching against the priests of his time with sovereign authority, accompanying his work with stupendous miracles, did not succeed to make a revolution, except some forty years after his death . . . and it was because his death gave an aureole to his teaching. On the contrary, my companions and fellow countrymen have attacked me as the records show, because they consider me impractical and a dreamer. I like first unity, the establishment of factories, industries, banks, and the like. For this reason I gave moral and material support to those who studied crafts and industries in Europe. I myself have spent much time studying ceramics, leather-tanning, manufacture of cement, and others. My dream was my country’s prosperity. I knew that through arms it was impossible to win liberties and less independence, for other colonizing nations like England, Germany, and others would not allow it. Japan would swallow us afterwards. But with a prosperous and enlightened people, liberties would not have to wait. I would like the Filipino people to become worthy, noble, honorable, for a people who makes itself despicable for its cowardice or vices exposes itself to abuses and vexations. Man in general oppresses what he despises, and this is what I used to say to those who complained to me: “If we were more worthy, they would not do that to us.” And this is my sorrow now, seeing how ignoble are those who, in order to accuse me, do not hesitate to lie unashamedly.

Forensic Analysis of the Metaphor

This is the most striking passage of the memorandum. Rizal reverses cause and effect: “separatist ideas are not mine; rather I am their effect.” He was the visible symptom of colonial grievance, not its cause. By deporting him in 1892, Spain closed the “safety valve,” and the disease turned inward. The passage also states his real program — unity, factories, banks, education — and his realism that armed revolt could not win against the colonial powers.

K § XI
Loyalty • Education & Foreign Offers

Part K: A Spanish Education, Foreign Protection Refused

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

I have never believed, nor can I believe, that these aspirations of mine are criminal in the eyes of the Government. They are the aspirations that my eminently Spanish education, and as such patriotic, has evolved in me. Since childhood I have been educated among Spaniards, I have been nurtured with the great examples in the history of Spain, Greece, and Rome; later in Spain, my professors have all been great thinkers, great patriots. Books, magazines, examples, reason—all made me love the welfare of my native land, as the Catalan loves the welfare of Cataluña, the Vascongado, the Galician, the Andalucian, Vizcaya, Galicia, Andalucía, respectively, etc. So remote it was for me to believe that I was doing wrong, that never did I like to accept the protection of another nation. Twice I was offered German nationality and once English, and I never accepted either. So confident I was in my right to aspire to what I believe to be just that whenever I had an opportunity, I have come to the Philippines. Mr. José Taviel de Andrade expressed to me his surprise upon seeing me in the Philippines, the same did Governor Ordas y Avecilla. I replied that I saw no reason why. During the seven months that I was free in the Philippines (1887-1888) I had caused no tumult whatsoever. The only question that occurred was an inquiry of the Administration of Finance which was answered in accordance with the facts, contrary to what other persons desired.5

A long time ago, July 1887, certain Japanese personages asked me why we did not rise up, saying that they would help us, etc., etc. I answered them that we were happy with Spain and we did not wish to pass from one hand to another, that we were bound to Spain, in spite of everything, by three centuries, a common religion, and ties of affection and gratitude, things we did not have with any other nation. They replied that Japan had no interest whatsoever in the Philippines and she would help only for racial reasons. I smiled and I proved to them through history that their ancestors did not think as they did. This is absolutely true.

Defense Significance

Rizal argues good faith: his love of country is the same regional patriotism a Catalan or Galician feels within Spain. His conduct proves it — he twice declined German nationality and once English, refused Japanese offers of help, and kept returning to the Philippines openly. José Taviel de Andrade, mentioned here, was the lieutenant assigned as his bodyguard in 1887 — and the brother of Luis Taviel de Andrade, the officer now defending him.

L § XII
★ Closing Argument • “Condemn Me”

Part L: “If This Is Punishable, Condemn Me”

“
"Now, if I’m to be punished or condemned for having desired the unity of my fellow countrymen, the welfare of my country, her material development… if this is punishable, condemn me."
— Dr. José Rizal, Data for My Defense, December 12, 1896

Rizal’s Own Words (Verbatim Text)

This is my way of thinking. I cannot deny that sometimes rebellious and punishable ideas have crossed my imagination, especially when my family was being persecuted, but afterwards reflection, the reality of facts, the absurdity of the thought, made me recover my senses, because I don’t believe I’m stupid or foolish to want an impossible and senseless thing.

Now, if I’m to be punished or condemned for having desired the unity of my fellow countrymen, the welfare of my country, her material development, her equality as far as possible to the provinces in Spain in order not to be called colony, if this is punishable, condemn me.

For rebellion or as its accomplice, or for having believed it to be possible or feasible, never: Not even one day’s detention for this reason; it would be an unjust detention.

Forensic Analysis of the Closing

Because this memo was meant only for his lawyer, Rizal is unusually candid: he admits that rebellious thoughts once crossed his mind when his family was persecuted (the Calamba evictions), then explains how reason dismissed them. He then draws the decisive legal line between aspiration and crime: he will accept punishment for wanting unity, prosperity, and provincial equality — but for rebellion, complicity, or even believing rebellion feasible, “never: not even one day’s detention.”

CARTA AL DEFENSOR • A PERSONAL APPEAL TO COUNSEL

Synthesis: From Private Memorandum to Formal Brief

Data for My Defense (December 12) was written for one reader — his lawyer — and so it is frank, discursive, and personal, even admitting that rebellious thoughts once crossed his mind. Its core arguments — the leader who is never obeyed, liberty versus independence, the Liga’s early death, the missing letters — reappear, sharpened and numbered, in the twelve points of Adiciones a Mi Defensa (December 26), addressed to the Council of War.

01 I
Political Abstention & Counsel Against Revolt

Point 1: Absolute Political Abstention in Dapitan & Repudiation of Armed Revolt to Dr. Valenzuela

Prosecution's Accusation

That Rizal served as the continuous intellectual instigator, supreme head, and spiritual organizer of the separatist revolution while in Dapitan.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal established that from July 1892 through July 1896, he completely refrained from political engagement. When Katipunan emissary Dr. Pío Valenzuela was dispatched to Dapitan to notify him of the planned uprising, Rizal explicitly and vehemently counselled against armed revolt, stating that a rebellion without weapons, ships, and international recognition was suicide.

Forensic Corroboration: Dr. Valenzuela himself left Dapitan convinced by Rizal’s counsel. Upon his return to Manila, instead of leading or joining armed skirmishes, Valenzuela immediately surrendered to Spanish authorities—proving that Rizal's words had actively suppressed, rather than encouraged, the revolt.
02 II
Absence of Secret Correspondence

Point 2: The Incongruity of Sending an Unknown Emissary Under an Assumed Identity

Prosecution's Accusation

That clandestine communication networks linked Rizal directly to the high command of the Katipunan conspirators.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal pointed out the glaring logistical contradiction: if secret communication lines or codes had existed between him and the conspirators, sending Dr. Valenzuela under a false name ("Procopio Bonifacio") at enormous expense would have been absurdly unnecessary.

Forensic Corroboration: On the very same steamer that brought Valenzuela to Dapitan were five of Rizal's close family members (his sister and nieces). If Rizal had an ongoing clandestine channel, familiar couriers would have been utilized. Sending an unknown stranger proved that the organizers knew Rizal was completely in the dark regarding their plans. Furthermore, despite extensive searches, not a single letter from Rizal encouraging revolt was ever produced by the prosecution.
03 III
Voluntary Conduct & Clear Conscience

Point 3: Rejection of Rescue Offers, Cuba Medical Service, & Unconditional Surrender

Prosecution's Accusation

That Rizal was plotting sedition and attempting to evade Spanish justice under cover of medical duties.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal documented his unimpeachable voluntary conduct:

  • He explicitly rejected clandestine offers to help him escape from Dapitan.
  • He voluntarily petitioned the Governor-General to serve as an unpaid volunteer military surgeon in the hospitals of Cuba.
  • When brought to Manila Bay in August 1896, he placed himself unconditionally under Gov. Gen. Blanco’s command aboard the cruiser Castilla for nearly a month, rather than attempting escape.
  • During his steamer’s coaling layover in British-governed Singapore, where English law protected political refugees and friends urged him to jump ship, he refused to escape, insisting that a clear conscience needs no flight.
04 IV
Opportunities to Escape Ignored

Point 4: Unfettered Coastal Freedom in Dapitan: Investment in Land vs. Fleeing via Moro Vintas

Prosecution's Accusation

That Dapitan was used as a subversive clandestine command post from which an armed breakout was planned.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal exposed the sheer illogicality of the accusation. In Dapitan, he enjoyed substantial freedom: he owned boats, was permitted to travel unaccompanied along the rugged coasts for days at a time, and visited remote rancherias without armed escorts.

The Concrete Counter-Proof: If he harbored revolutionary ambitions or feared arrest, he could have effortlessly slipped away to Borneo or the Dutch East Indies aboard swift Moro vintas. Instead, he did the precise opposite: he bought real estate, planted agricultural crops, engineered waterworks, constructed a hospital, established a school for local youths, and brought his aged mother and sisters to reside permanently with him.
05 V
★ KEY LEGAL HIGHLIGHT • CHIEF PARADOX

Point 5: The Paradox of Leadership: "What Kind of a Chief is That?"

“
"What kind of a chief is that who is not consulted about the plans and is only informed so that he may escape? What kind of a chief is that who, when he says 'No', they say 'Yes'?"
— Dr. José Rizal, Adiciones a Mi Defensa, December 26, 1896

Forensic Analysis of Command & Control

This rhetorical and legal masterstroke demolished the prosecution's claim of military or operational command. Under any legal doctrine—military, colonial, or common law—a "leader" or "chief" exercises command responsibility, gives orders, and guides execution.

Rizal illustrated that the Katipunan treated his name as an unsolicited emblem without his consent or authority. When they approached him, it was not to seek strategic command, but merely to inform him of a fait accompli. When he exercised moral judgment and commanded them to desist ("No!"), they defied him and proceeded with rebellion ("Yes!"). Ergo, he possessed zero command authority over the Katipunan.

06 VI
Purposes of La Liga Filipina

Point 6: Statutory Intent of La Liga: Commerce, Industry, and Union

Prosecution's Accusation

That La Liga Filipina was an illegal, seditious association founded deliberately to prepare the population for armed insurrection against the Spanish Crown.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal pointed to the explicit statutory articles he drafted for La Liga Filipina. The organization was legally modelled on European civic cooperation and mutual-aid societies. Its enumerated objectives were:

  • Promotion of domestic commerce and local manufacture;
  • Mutual economic defense against injustice and poverty;
  • Encouragement of scientific and agricultural education;
  • Civic union and fraternity among Filipinos under the Spanish realm.
Witness Testimony: Even the hostile witnesses presented by the prosecution—when questioned about the founding meeting in Tondo—were forced to concede under oath that Rizal spoke solely about economic enterprise, education, and moral solidarity, not rebellion.
07 VII
Immediate Dissolution of Liga

Point 7: The "Single Meeting" Reality: Extinction of the Original Liga in 1892

Prosecution's Accusation

That La Liga was a permanent, continuous revolutionary conspiracy that matured directly into the uprising of 1896.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal established the chronological fact of the Liga’s immediate demise. The organization held exactly one solitary founding meeting on July 3, 1892. Days later, on July 6, Rizal was incarcerated in Fort Santiago and promptly deported to Dapitan.

The Fact of Extinction: Without leadership, funds, or executive structure, the original Liga became instantly inert and died on the spot in July 1892. An entity that ceased to function after one session cannot legally be deemed the operational vehicle for an insurrection four and a half years later.
08 VIII
Reorganization Without Knowledge

Point 8: Complete Ignorance of Subsequent Reorganization by Third Parties

Prosecution's Accusation

That Rizal continued to orchestrate the revived clandestine branches of La Liga in Manila through proxy leaders like Domingo Franco and Apolinario Mabini.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal testified that he was completely uninformed about any subsequent attempt by others to resurrect or reorganize the Liga nine months later in April 1893. While confined in Dapitan under strict Spanish military surveillance and postal censorship, he was neither notified nor asked for approval.

Principle of Individual Liability: In criminal jurisprudence, an individual cannot be held criminally culpable for unauthorized actions undertaken by third parties in distant provinces without his knowledge, consent, or participation.
09 IX
Katipunan vs. Liga Argument

Point 9: Institutional Antagonism: Why the Katipunan Was Founded to Replace the Liga

Prosecution's Accusation

That La Liga Filipina and the Katipunan were indistinguishable iterations of the same seditious conspiracy under Rizal.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal dissected the institutional incompatibility between the two entities. The Katipunan (KKK) was founded by Andrés Bonifacio on July 7, 1892—the very night Rizal's deportation to Dapitan was announced—precisely because Bonifacio and the radicals recognized that La Liga Filipina was strictly peaceful, bourgeois, and totally inadequate for revolutionary combat.

The Branding Proof: If La Liga had served revolutionary aims, or if Rizal had been their commanding chief, the revolutionaries would have eagerly retained and capitalized upon the prestige of the Liga’s name and structure. Instead, they discarded the Liga entirely and created a fundamentally different, clandestine militant order with blood oaths and combat lodges.
10 X
Historical Context of Acrimonious Letters

Point 10: Human Context Behind Bitter Letters: The Calamba Agrarian Evictions

Prosecution's Accusation

Private correspondence from the early 1890s was submitted containing biting, angry remarks against Spanish friars and colonial administrators as proof of habitual treasonous intent.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal urged the court to evaluate private correspondence within its true temporal and human context. Those sharp, indignant letters were penned during 1889–1891, when the Dominican hacienda management in Calamba dispossessed his family:

  • His elderly parents were forcibly evicted from their ancestral home;
  • Their family houses and storehouses were demolished under military order;
  • His brother Paciano and brothers-in-law were arrested and deported to remote islands (Bohol, Mindoro, and the Marianas).
Juridical Distinction: Anguished grief and familial indignation over administrative cruelties cannot be conflated with organizing a criminal armed conspiracy against the sovereignty of the State years later.
11 XI
Exemplary Conduct in Exile

Point 11: Four Years of Resigned, Exemplary Conduct Certified by Military Commanders

Prosecution's Accusation

That throughout his exile, Rizal was secretly subverting colonial authority and agitating the native population against Spain.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

Rizal appealed to the official state records themselves. For four continuous years (1892–1896), he lived in resigned acceptance of his sentence, dedicating every hour to civil, medical, and scientific enrichment.

Official Spanish Verification: The Spanish politico-military commandants of Dapitan—specifically Captain Ricardo Carnicero and Captain Juan Sitges—repeatedly submitted official reports praising Rizal's irreproachable personal conduct, submission to law, and civic diligence. Even local Jesuit priests, despite fundamental religious debates with Rizal, acknowledged his gentlemanly integrity and total lack of subversive agitation.
12 XII
The "One Night" Fallacy & Final Rebuttal

Point 12: The "One Night" Fallacy: Can an Entire Revolution Be Organized in a Single Evening?

Prosecution's Accusation

That during the single meeting on July 3, 1892 at the house of Doroteo Ongjunco, Rizal laid down the entire foundation for the armed revolution that exploded in August 1896.

Rizal's Legal Rebuttal

In this climactic rebuttal, Rizal challenged the Council of War with devastating common sense and legal causality:

"How could I have organized an entire revolution in one night alone, during a meeting where only commerce and mutual aid were discussed? If attendees truly took my words as revolutionary, why did the Liga collapse immediately rather than mobilize?"
The Concluding Emissary Proof: Rizal closed by drawing attention to the very people sent to Dapitan. Why did the Katipunan send Dr. Valenzuela—a complete stranger whom Rizal had never met—to solicit his approval? Because his actual lifelong friends, intellectual peers, and confidants were intimately aware that José Rizal was irrevocably opposed to violent, premature insurrection. Only those who did not know him could dream he would support the revolt.

JURISPRUDENCE FOR THE 21ST CENTURY

Modern Relevance & Civic Lessons

Far from being a quaint relic of the 19th century, Rizal's defense brief provides enduring principles for modern civil society, constitutionalism, and information integrity.

01

Due Process & Fair Trial

Guilt must be established by verified, demonstrable evidence—never through mere association, unsubstantiated rumor, or convenient political assumptions. Rizal’s refusal to accept vague allegations remains a foundational cornerstone of universal procedural rights.

Evidence vs. Association
02

Critical Thinking Under Crisis

Demonstrates how to confront life-threatening, high-stakes accusations with cool, methodical logic and forensic proof rather than paralyzing emotional panic. Even under the shadow of the firing squad, Rizal cataloged dates, travel manifests, and statutory texts.

Methodical Reason
03

Integrity & Standing on Principle

Rizal defended objective truth and intellectual honesty even while knowing that the military court-martial was predetermined and politically rigged. He refused to confess to falsehoods or flatter authorities to obtain clemency at the expense of his honor.

Intellectual Honesty
04

Trial by Rumor in the Digital Age

Highlights the perennial peril of convicting individuals on unverified hearsay and fabricated narratives. This historical injustice mirrors modern digital trials: algorithm-driven "cancel culture", coordinated disinformation, and online mob justice without scrutiny.

Disinformation & Echo Chambers
05

The Predetermined Verdict Problem

Gov. Gen. Polavieja had effectively decided the outcome before the trial concluded—a reality Rizal understood yet still chose to argue with intellectual rigor. This mirrors modern authoritarian show-trials where the appearance of legal process masks a predetermined political result. The existence of a formal defense is not sufficient proof of genuine due process.

Judicial Independence

Synthesis: The Complete Intellectual

History predominantly venerates Rizal as the martyr-poet who composed Mi Último Adiós on the eve of his execution. However, Adiciones a Mi Defensa reveals the other hemisphere of his genius: a disciplined intellect capable of meeting an imperial military tribunal on the battlefield of pure legal logic and procedural fairness.
